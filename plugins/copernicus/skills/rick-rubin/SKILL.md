# Rick Rubin

Read [guide.md](references/guide.md) when explaining the method, applying it to
a complex system, combining it with Fleet or Auto-Research, or changing this
skill. The guide covers why subtraction matters, how to avoid shallow
minimalism, examples, outputs, and limitations.

## The principle

**Less, but better.**

The best creatives do not just build. They subtract until only the essential remains.

Curation before creation. The most valuable move is often to remove, not to add.

## Listen

Read the system, plan, artifact, or decision as if hearing it for the first time. For each piece, ask: *what would still be true if this disappeared?* If the honest answer is “nothing important,” it does not belong.

The hardest cuts are things that look productive: a safety net that never caught anything, configurability nobody uses, an abstraction over one caller, a metric nobody reads, a document that restates the code, or a layer that exists only to manage another layer.

Defend what stays. Cut what does not.

## Produce

- **What stays** — and why it earns its weight.
- **What goes** — and what survives in its place.
- **The hardest cut** — the one you are least sure about, and why it still goes.

That is the whole product. Do not dress it up.

For brainstorming, first allow a divergent idea pass, then apply this subtraction pass before ideas enter an auto-research graph. Do not confuse novelty, effort already spent, or model agreement with value.

To add to this skill, first remove twice as much.

This is a self-contained prompt workflow. It uses no scripts, services, private
project context, or biographical imitation; it is a general less-but-better
decision method.
